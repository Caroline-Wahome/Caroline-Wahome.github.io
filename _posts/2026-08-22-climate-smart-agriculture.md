# Hands-on Climate-Smart Agriculture: Lessons from Farm Management, Resource Efficiency and Farmer Engagement

My understanding of climate-smart agriculture has been shaped not only by academic research but also by hands-on experience working within agricultural production systems. My experience as Managing Coordinator at Alan Keegan Farm in Ngong provided an important practical foundation for understanding how resource management, crop production, environmental stewardship and market considerations come together within a working farm.

*Hands-on engagement with sustainable agricultural production and resource management.*

## Efficient resource management

One of the most important lessons from working within a farm production system is that productivity depends on how efficiently available resources are managed.

Land, water, labour, inputs and time all have to be carefully coordinated to maintain production while minimising unnecessary losses. Efficient resource use is therefore not simply an environmental consideration. It is also central to farm productivity and economic sustainability.

This practical experience strengthened my appreciation of the need to optimise agricultural systems rather than treating individual production challenges in isolation.

## Water conservation and responsible use

Water is one of the most important resources in agricultural production, particularly in systems that depend on irrigation.

Efficient water management requires understanding crop requirements, irrigation practices, timing and losses within the production system. Water conservation therefore becomes an important component of building agricultural systems that can remain productive under increasing environmental pressure.

My interest in sustainable agriculture has continued to develop around the broader question of how agricultural production can be maintained while using natural resources responsibly.

## Crop health and disease management

Maintaining healthy crops is another essential component of efficient agricultural production.

Disease management requires attention to crop health throughout the production cycle rather than responding only when disease problems become severe. This includes monitoring crops, understanding environmental conditions that influence disease development and applying appropriate management practices.

My background in microbiology has provided an additional perspective on crop health, particularly through understanding the interactions between microorganisms, plants and their surrounding environment.

## Post-harvest handling

Agricultural productivity does not end when a crop is harvested.

Post-harvest handling is critical because losses occurring after harvest can significantly reduce the value of agricultural production. Appropriate harvesting, handling, sorting, storage and transportation practices can help maintain product quality and reduce avoidable losses.

This has reinforced my view that sustainable agriculture needs to consider the entire production chain, from resource use and crop establishment through to harvesting, post-harvest management and markets.

## Connecting production with markets

For farmers, producing a good crop is only part of the equation.

Market access and reliable market links are important for ensuring that agricultural production translates into improved livelihoods. Understanding what markets require, when products are needed and how quality influences value can help farmers make better production decisions.

This perspective has shaped my interest in agricultural systems that connect production decisions with economic realities and opportunities for farmers.

## Learning through engagement with smallholder farmers

Some of the most valuable insights into agricultural challenges come through direct interaction with farmers.

My work has involved engaging with smallholder farmers and participating in capacity-building activities across rural Kenya. These experiences have provided opportunities to exchange knowledge, understand practical challenges and discuss approaches to sustainable agricultural production.

Between 2019 and 2023, I co-led training workshops in Kisii, Nyamira and Embu counties focusing on beneficial microorganisms, low-cost plant propagation and sustainable cultivation practices.

I have also represented Kenyatta University at national ASK Trade Fairs, engaging with farmers and other stakeholders around sustainable agricultural technologies.

These interactions have reinforced the importance of recognising farmers as partners in innovation rather than simply recipients of technologies developed elsewhere.

## Linking practical agriculture with research

My practical agricultural experience has continued to inform my scientific research.

My background in microbiology has allowed me to explore agricultural systems from a biological perspective, including beneficial microorganisms, rhizosphere microbial communities, crop production and soil health.

My doctoral research on banana rhizosphere microbial diversity, tissue culture banana production and acclimatization using arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi further developed this connection between agricultural practice and microbiological research.

My current work on environmental monitoring and zero-waste biosensors builds on this broader interest in developing accessible approaches that can provide useful information for environmental and agricultural decision-making.

## A systems perspective on climate-smart agriculture

For me, climate-smart agriculture is not a single technology or practice.

It is a systems approach that considers how resources are managed, how crops are produced, how diseases are controlled, how water is conserved, how losses are reduced after harvest, how farmers access markets and how knowledge is shared between researchers and agricultural communities.

My hands-on experience has therefore shaped my interest in agricultural approaches that are productive, resource-efficient, environmentally responsible and responsive to the realities of farmers.

## Looking ahead

I am particularly interested in approaches that bring together practical agricultural knowledge, environmental microbiology, soil health, resource efficiency, accessible technologies and stakeholder engagement.

The future of sustainable agriculture will require stronger connections between scientific research and practical experience. It will also require continued dialogue among researchers, farmers, institutions, businesses and other stakeholders.

My work continues to explore these connections, with the goal of contributing to agricultural systems that are productive, sustainable and better equipped to respond to changing environmental conditions.
